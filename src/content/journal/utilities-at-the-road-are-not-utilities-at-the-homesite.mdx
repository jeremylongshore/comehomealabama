A buyer can stand on a Baldwin County parcel, see houses across the road, and assume water and sewer are handled. That assumption can survive right up to the first permit call.

The utility line may belong to a different provider. Sewer may be nearby but have no available connection for that parcel. The lot may need a grinder pump, a long private service run, a septic system, or a well. Those are different projects with different bills.

Before you price the house, price the path from the road to the house.

## Start with the parcel number, not the street name

South Baldwin County is a patchwork of municipal, cooperative, public, and private utility territories. A Foley mailing address does not prove the property is inside Foley, and a Gulf Shores address does not tell you which company serves the dirt.

Call the planning or building office for the parcel's actual jurisdiction first. Ask which water and wastewater providers serve that parcel, then contact each provider directly. Baldwin County's current residential permit checklist requires a sewer release from the utility provider or a septic release from the Baldwin County Health Department. It also requires a water release.

That sequence matters. A map, roadside marker, or neighbor's connection is a lead. A written release or availability answer tied to the parcel is evidence.

## If sewer is available, ask what “available” includes

Baldwin County Sewer Service is a private utility, not a county department, and it provides sewer rather than water. Other parts of the county use other providers. That is why “county sewer” is too vague to build a budget around.

Ask the provider five questions in writing:

1. Is service available to this exact parcel today?
2. Where is the connection point, and how far is it from the planned homesite?
3. Is the connection gravity-fed or a low-pressure system with a grinder pump?
4. What tap, impact, application, installation, and monthly charges apply now?
5. Which equipment and service lines will the owner maintain after construction?

On a low-pressure system, Baldwin County Sewer Service says each connected building needs a grinder pump. Its installation guidance also says power, water, wiring, and plumbing must be ready before pump startup. So “sewer is on the road” may still leave trenching, electrical work, plumbing, final grading, and scheduling on the owner's side of the worksheet.

Get the boundary of responsibility in writing. The expensive sentence is often the one that begins, “The owner is responsible from here.”

## If septic is the answer, test the soil before the offer goes hard

Alabama law requires a local health department permit before a new onsite sewage system is installed. In Baldwin County, the health department tells the owner to begin with a test by a registered soils professional. The results go in with the application and fee. If the site has restrictions, a registered engineer must design the system.

After review and a site visit, the health department can issue the permit. A licensed installer then does the work, and the system must be inspected before it is covered.

That is more than a quick “perc test passed” conversation. Ask for the soil report, the proposed system type, the usable disposal area, the repair area, and a plan showing how both fit with the house, driveway, well, setbacks, drainage, and any wetlands. A large parcel can still have a small workable building envelope once every piece gets drawn.

If the seller has an old approval, confirm it still applies to the house size and placement you intend to build. Adding bedrooms can also trigger a new sewer or health department release under the county's permit checklist.

## A proposed well still needs its own due diligence

A private well is not free water. It is a drilling, pump, power, treatment, testing, and maintenance decision.

Alabama's environmental agency says it does not regulate individual private wells or maintain a complete database of them. It points owners with construction concerns to a certified well driller and owners with water-quality concerns to the health department or a certified drinking-water laboratory. The Alabama Department of Public Health recommends routine testing and says private-well owners are responsible for making sure their water is safe.

Before closing, get a written drilling estimate based on the parcel, ask what is included if the first depth does not produce the needed supply, and budget the pump, pressure tank, electrical run, treatment equipment, and laboratory testing separately. If an existing well is part of the sale, test the water and have the equipment evaluated. A faucet that runs during a showing does not answer either question.

## Put all four paths on one sheet

Do not compare only “sewer versus septic” or “public water versus well.” Compare the complete path:

- provider confirmation and releases;
- connection, tap, and impact charges;
- trenching from the connection point to the homesite;
- grinder pump or septic design and installation;
- well drilling, pump, treatment, and testing;
- electrical work and backup-power needs;
- monthly charges and owner-maintained equipment;
- time required before the building permit can move.

Then place the utility plan on the same survey as the house, driveway, drainage, and flood-zone work. If the pieces compete for the same ground, you need to know while your offer can still protect you.

Land can be a good buy with a well and septic system. It can also be a good buy with a long sewer connection. The honest answer comes from knowing which project you are buying before the dirt becomes yours.
