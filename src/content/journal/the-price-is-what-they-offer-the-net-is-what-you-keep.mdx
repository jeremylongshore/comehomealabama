This time of year I get a lot of calls that start the same way. A seller has an offer, or two,
and they want to know which one is better. Almost always they're looking at the price.

The price matters. But the number that matters more is the one that lands in your account
after closing, and on this coast a few things can move it further than people expect. Here's
what comes out between a signed contract and a recorded deed, and how to see it before you
sign anything.

## Start with the payoff, not the price

Your mortgage balance on last month's statement isn't your payoff. Interest keeps running
until the day the loan is paid, and the payoff statement shows the exact amount through a
specific date. Federal rules require your servicer to send an accurate payoff statement no
more than seven business days after a written request, so ask early. Your closing agent will
order it too, but you want to see it before you're comparing offers.

If there's a second mortgage or a home equity line, it gets paid off and closed at the same
table. A line of credit you forgot was open still shows up on the title search.

## What comes out at the closing table

Most of the deductions are predictable once you know where to look:

- **Commission.** Whatever you agreed to in your listing agreement, plus any compensation you
  agreed to offer the buyer's agent. It's figured on the price, so a higher offer costs a
  little more here.
- **Title and settlement charges.** Who pays which pieces is set by your contract, not by a
  rule everybody follows. Read that section before you sign.
- **Transfer tax.** More on this below, because the state line changes it a lot.
- **Property taxes.** Taxes get split between you and the buyer by date, and any bill that's
  due and unpaid comes out of your side. Property tax bills went out on both sides of the line
  this fall, so ask whether yours has been paid.
- **Dues and assessments.** HOA or condo dues are settled through the closing date. If your
  association has voted an assessment that isn't fully paid, ask how your contract handles it.
  That one surprises sellers more than anything else on this list.
- **Credits and repairs.** Anything you agreed to after the inspection, plus any closing-cost
  help the buyer asked for in the offer.

## Two states, two very different transfer taxes

This is where selling on the Alabama side and the Florida side really part ways.

Alabama's deed tax is 50 cents for every $500 of value. On a $450,000 cash sale that's $450,
and it can be less, because the law only taxes the value above a mortgage recorded with it.

Florida's documentary stamp tax on a deed is 70 cents for every $100. The same $450,000 sale
on Perdido Key carries $3,150 in doc stamps.

Who pays it is a contract question on either side, so I won't tell you it's always yours. But
if you're selling in Florida, that line deserves a look before you count your proceeds.

## Compare offers by what they leave you

Here's an illustration, not a real deal. Say you get two offers on a house in Foley.

- **Offer A:** $460,000, financed, and the buyer asks for $12,000 toward closing costs.
- **Offer B:** $452,000, cash, no credit.

Offer A looks $8,000 better. Take out the credit and it's $448,000 against $452,000, before
anything else comes off. Offer A also pays a slightly bigger commission because the price is
higher, and it carries a lender, an appraisal and an insurance binder that all have to land
on time. None of that makes Offer B automatically right. A financed buyer at a solid number
can be a great deal. It just means the comparison has to happen on the net, not the headline.

## Money that comes back after closing

Two things tend to show up later, and sellers forget about both.

**Your escrow balance.** If your lender held money for taxes and insurance, federal rules
require the servicer to return what's left within 20 business days after the loan is paid in
full. Watch the mail.

**Your insurance.** Keep your homeowners, wind and flood policies in force until the deed
records. Once it does, call each carrier, cancel, and ask about a refund of the unused
premium. Cancel early and you're the owner of an uninsured house on the coast, which is
exactly the wrong time to save a month's premium.

## If you're a foreign seller for U.S. tax purposes

This one is about tax status, and it's easy to miss. If the IRS treats you as a foreign
seller, the buyer generally has to withhold 15 percent of the amount realized and send it to the IRS. One
exception covers a buyer who will live in the property as a residence when the price is
$300,000 or less, and the rules for that are specific. If this applies to you, talk to a tax
professional before you list, not the week of closing.

## Ask for a net sheet before you sign

Before you accept any offer, ask your agent to run a net sheet on it, and on every offer you're
comparing. It should show:

1. The payoff, from an actual payoff statement or a recent balance plus interest
2. Commission as your agreements set it
3. Title, settlement and transfer tax charges as the contract assigns them
4. Property taxes and dues through the closing date, plus any unpaid assessment
5. Every credit, concession and repair dollar in the offer
6. The bottom line, side by side for each offer

None of this is legal or tax advice, and your closing agent's final statement is the number
that counts. But you should never have to guess what you're walking away with.
