A house can look completely dry at two in the afternoon and still have a water problem. That is not a reason to avoid every low-looking yard from Foley to Perdido Key. It is a reason to slow down long enough to learn where the water goes when the coast gets a real rain.

September is a useful month for that kind of homework. A brief shower can show you more than an immaculate showing ever will. On a house near Fort Morgan Road, in an older part of Gulf Shores, or farther inland around Foley, the drainage pattern is part of the property. Put it in the same category as the roof, the electrical panel, and the survey.

## Start at the curb

Before you walk inside, stand at the street and look both ways. Is there a swale, curb inlet, ditch, or culvert? Is it clear enough to carry water, or is it buried under leaves, sand, and somebody's landscaping? Then look at the driveway. A driveway that bridges a ditch may need a culvert, and a culvert is only useful if water can enter and leave it.

You are not trying to diagnose the whole neighborhood from the curb. You are looking for questions to carry into inspection. Ask the seller for any drainage work, permits, invoices, or warranty information they have. Ask whether water has ever come into the garage, porch, or enclosed storage area. Get the answer in writing with the rest of the disclosure record, then let your inspector look at the evidence rather than a polished verbal answer.

## Read the grade, not the grass

Fresh sod can make almost any yard look level. Walk from the house toward the property lines and notice the slope. Does it move away from the slab? Does a patio or pool deck send runoff back toward the wall? Are downspouts carrying roof water several feet away, or dumping it beside the foundation?

On the coast, landscaping changes hands often. A new bed, a raised paver edge, or a load of fill can redirect water without looking dramatic. That does not mean the house has a defect. It means the answer should be specific: where does water collect, how long does it stay, and what has been done to move it?

If the lot is near a canal, bay, or marsh edge, add the survey and elevation information to that conversation. A flood map and a drainage path are related, but they are not the same question. The map helps identify the mapped flood hazard. The yard walk helps you understand the day-to-day water story around this particular house.

## Use the inspection window well

Tell your inspector before the appointment that drainage is a concern. A general inspection may identify visible grading, gutter, moisture, or site-drainage conditions, but it is not a substitute for a drainage engineer, surveyor, or contractor when the signs point to a larger issue. If the inspector sees standing water marks, settlement, a failing culvert, or water moving toward the house, ask what specialist can answer the next question.

That sequence matters. Do not order a costly opinion just because a neighbor's yard is wet after a storm. Do order the right opinion when the inspection gives you a concrete reason. Your contract deadlines are there to protect the decision, not to make the week more exciting.

For a house in unincorporated Baldwin County, confirm the permitting jurisdiction by parcel number before assuming the county handles every issue. City limits, municipal drainage, and private subdivision responsibilities can split the answer. The same is true across the Alabama-Florida line: a Pensacola address can involve a different local office than a Perdido Key address a few minutes away.

## Put insurance in the same folder

Water questions should travel with your insurance questions. Send the address, year built, roof information, and elevation certificate if one exists to an insurance agent as soon as you are under contract. Ask what coverage is required by the lender, what is optional, and which deductibles would apply to the policy being quoted. Do not wait for the last week of closing, especially during hurricane season, to find out whether a carrier needs more information.

There is no prize for buying the house that looked best for twenty minutes. The better purchase is the one whose roof, drainage, insurance, and monthly number all still make sense after you have asked the plain questions.
