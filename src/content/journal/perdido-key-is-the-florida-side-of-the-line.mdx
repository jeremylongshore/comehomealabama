Most people find Perdido Key by accident. They're driving east out of Orange Beach, pass the
Flora-Bama, and suddenly the signs say Florida. The beach looks the same. The sand is the same
sugar-white. But the minute you cross that line, the rules that govern what you're buying
change, and that's the part nobody mentions at the open house.

## What Perdido Key actually is

Perdido Key is a barrier island in unincorporated Escambia County, Florida. Perdido Key Drive
runs its length, and the Theo Baars Bridge carries you over the Intracoastal Waterway to
Innerarity Point and the rest of west Pensacola. There's no city government on the key. Your
county, your building department and your property appraiser are all Escambia County.

A lot of the island will never be built on. Perdido Key State Park holds one stretch of dunes,
and the Perdido Key Area of Gulf Islands National Seashore holds the eastern end. Per the
National Park Service, you can drive Johnson Beach Road about a mile and a half to a turnaround.
Past that, the island is reachable only on foot or by boat. For an owner, that matters: a big
share of the shoreline near you is public land, not the next condo tower.

What's left to own is mostly Gulf-front and bay-side condominiums, plus a smaller supply of
single-family homes and townhomes on and just off the key.

## The beach mouse is a real line item

This is the detail a tourist couldn't fake. Perdido Key is home to the Perdido Key beach mouse,
which Escambia County describes as critically endangered and protected under the Endangered
Species Act. The county holds a federal incidental take permit under its Perdido Key Habitat
Conservation Plan, and per its own page, every parcel containing critical or suitable beach
mouse habitat, as determined by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, is required to hold a Beach
Mouse Permit.

Here's what that permit asks for, according to the county: a habitat management plan, a land
disturbance permit and impact fees paid in full, full site plans showing lighting fixtures and
landscaping, two nights of trapping by a qualified technician before any land is disturbed,
deed restrictions recorded before the building permit, and conservation easements recorded
before the certificate of occupancy. The beach mouse permit has to be issued before the county
building permit. Permits are first-come, first-served and capped by five-year allocations per
zoning category.

The county program spares owners a direct federal application, which it says can take up to
two years. But if you're buying a lot or planning a teardown, confirm the zoning and habitat acreage with
the county before you price the build, not after.

## Florida's beach rules ride along

On the Gulf side, there's a second line to know. Florida's Coastal Construction Control Line
marks a zone where the state applies special siting and design rules to protect the beach and
dunes. The state's Department of Environmental Protection publishes a map viewer showing where a
parcel sits. If you're planning a pool, a deck, a dune walkover or anything
new on the Gulf side, check the map first and plan for a state permit on top of the county ones.

## Condos here carry Florida's inspection law

This is the section I'd read twice if you're shopping condos. Under section 553.899 of the
Florida Statutes, condominium buildings three habitable stories or taller need a milestone
inspection by December 31 of the year they turn 30, then every 10 years after. The law lets the
local building department move that to 25 years based on local conditions, including closeness
to salt water. On a barrier island, ask which one applies to your building.

Phase one is a visual look by an architect or engineer. If it finds substantial structural
deterioration, phase two follows, and repairs have to begin within 365 days of that report.
Within 45 days of getting the report, the association has to give every owner a copy of the
inspector's summary, post it on the property and publish the full report on its website if it's
required to have one.

What that means for a buyer is simple. Before you write an offer on a Perdido Key condo, ask
for:

1. The building's age and its milestone inspection status, including which year applies.
2. The inspector's summary and, if there was one, the phase two report.
3. Any repair plan, its schedule and how the association is paying for it.
4. The reserve study and the current budget, so you can see whether repairs are funded or
   headed for a special assessment.
5. An insurance quote on the unit itself, with the deductible written in dollars.

None of this makes a building good or bad. It tells you what you're actually buying.

## What to go in eyes-open about

The state line cuts both ways. Florida has no state income tax, and Alabama taxes owner-occupied
homes very differently, so the honest comparison depends on your income and the house. I walked
through that math, side by side, in the journal's post on retiring along the Alabama-Florida
line. Don't assume either side wins until you run your own numbers.

Being on a barrier island also means one road in and out, and the same hurricane-season planning
as anywhere on this coast. Check the address on Florida's Know Your Zone map before you commit.
And remember that the public beaches are popular. The Park Service says the Perdido Key Area can
close at capacity from April through September, so summer weekends on the key are busy ones.
