Every fall I get the same call. Someone bought a wooded lot over the summer, the house plans are
coming together, and they want the pines down and the pile gone by Thanksgiving. Good news for
them: in Baldwin County, burning the debris from clearing land becomes legal again on November 1.

The less fun news is that the bulldozer is the last step, not the first. Before anything comes
down, there's a short stack of rules from the state and your city or the county. None of them are
hard. All of them are cheaper to follow than to fix.

## Why November matters

Alabama's open-burning rule lets you burn vegetation and untreated wood from clearing land for a
house. But the state revokes that permission every May through October in a dozen counties, and
Baldwin and Mobile are both on the list. So in practical terms, land-clearing burns here run
November through April.

That changes when to clear. Clear in winter and burning is an option. Clear in July and your
choices are hauling the debris off or letting it rot.

## The state's burn conditions

Even in season, the rule has conditions, and a few of them catch people off guard:

- **It has to burn where it grew.** You can't haul debris to another property and burn it there.
- **Five hundred feet from the nearest occupied house** that isn't on your property. On a lot
  inside an established subdivision, there's often a neighbor closer than that, which takes
  burning off the table no matter the month.
- **Vegetation and untreated wood only.** No treated lumber, no tires, no construction scraps, no
  household trash.
- **Start between 8 a.m. and 3 p.m.,** and add nothing after 3 p.m.
- **Mind the smoke.** It can't create a traffic hazard, and there's no burning under an air
  stagnation advisory or a governor's drought emergency.
- **Someone has to attend the fire** the whole time.

If the burn will cover a quarter acre or more, call the Alabama Forestry Commission's burn permit
line at (800) 392-5679 first. Cities can add their own limits; Gulf Shores has its own
open-burning permit application. Either way, whoever lit the fire stays responsible for damage
from the fire or the smoke.

The state's alternatives are plain: take the debris to a licensed landfill, or let it decompose
naturally. Sometimes that's the better choice even in winter.

## Clearing can need its own permit

This is the part buyers skip, because "it's my land" feels like it should settle it. It doesn't.

**Inside city limits, assume yes until told otherwise.** Gulf Shores issues a land disturbance
permit for clearing, cutting, excavating, filling or grading land. The application fee is $250
plus any other fees the city sets, with about a ten-day review. If a building permit has already
been issued, a separate land disturbance permit isn't required. Loxley adopted a land disturbance
ordinance this year that counts tree removal as land disturbance and applies to single-family
lots too. Every town writes its own version, so call the planning office that actually has the
parcel.

**Unincorporated Baldwin County depends on the district and the flood map.** Any land
disturbance in a flood zone, or an area the county treats as likely to flood, needs a county Flood
Land Disturbance Permit with an erosion-control plan. Zoned districts carry a 30-foot undisturbed setback from
wetlands, and some districts add their own native tree protections. If you want the deeper flood
side, here's [flood zone math on a Baldwin County lot](/journal/flood-zone-math-on-a-baldwin-county-lot/).

**One acre is the state's line.** If the clearing and grading will disturb an acre or more, the
project needs coverage under ADEM's construction stormwater permit, filed online before work
starts, with a fee. A smaller lot can still fall under it when it's part of a larger subdivision
plan of an acre or more.

## Wet ground changes everything

South Baldwin is flat, and plenty of wooded lots hold water in places. If any part might be
wetland, get a delineation before equipment goes in. If wetlands will be filled, the county wants
copies of the state and federal permits. Clearing first and asking later is the expensive order.

## The order I'd do it in

1. Confirm the jurisdiction: city limits, county planning district, and flood zone.
2. Ask that office whether clearing needs a permit, and what it costs.
3. Measure how much ground the pad, driveway and septic area will disturb.
4. If anything looks wet, get the wetlands answer on paper.
5. Choose the debris plan: burn in season, haul, or leave it to break down.
6. Put it in the clearing contract: who pulls the permits, who handles the debris, and how.

Under contract on land? Make that first call during your inspection period. It's easier to walk
away from a parcel than from a fine.

None of this is legal advice, and rules get rewritten, so check with the office that has
jurisdiction before the first tree comes down.
