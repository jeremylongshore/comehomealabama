Most first-time buyers I meet start with a price. They've been watching the apps, they know what a three-bedroom in Foley or Robertsdale tends to run, and they ask whether they can swing it.

That's a fair question. It's just the second question. On the coast, the first one is what the house costs every month, and the price is only part of that answer.

Buying here isn't harder than anywhere else. A few things are just different, and they tend to surface in week three of a contract instead of week one.

## Your monthly is four or five numbers, not one

The payment calculator on a listing site shows principal and interest, and maybe a guess at taxes. Near the water that leaves out the parts that move the most.

A real monthly here usually stacks up like this:

- **Principal and interest** on the loan.
- **Property taxes,** which run low in Baldwin County compared to a lot of the country, and differently on the Florida side.
- **Homeowners and wind coverage,** which in many coastal ZIP codes is the biggest surprise on the list.
- **Flood insurance,** required by your lender if the house sits in a high-risk flood zone, and worth pricing even when it isn't.
- **HOA or condo dues,** if there are any.

Two houses at the same price can land a few hundred dollars a month apart once insurance is in, because the year the roof was installed, how close the house is to the water, and the flood zone all change the quote. So before you fall for a house, get an insurance quote on that exact address. Your agent can get you the year built and roof details, and an insurance agent can usually turn a rough quote around in a day or two.

If the coverage layers are new to you, my earlier piece on how coastal insurance stacks walks through homeowners, wind and flood one at a time.

## Get pre-approved by someone who knows the coast

An online pre-approval is a start. A local lender who closes loans in Baldwin County every week is better, because they already know which questions underwriting will ask about insurance, flood zones and condos, and they'll ask them up front.

Ask your lender for a pre-approval built on a real insurance estimate, not a placeholder. A pre-approval that assumes a cheap policy is a number you'll lose in the middle of the deal.

## Look before you love a condo

A condo can look like the easy first home, and sometimes it is. But financing a condo depends on the building as well as on you. FHA and VA loans generally require the condo project itself to be approved, and conventional lenders review the association's budget, reserves, insurance and any litigation. A building with thin reserves or a pending special assessment can be hard or impossible to finance with the loan you're planning on.

So before you get attached to a unit, ask the lender whether the building works for your loan type. Then ask for the association's budget, the most recent reserve study and the history of any assessments. Those three documents tell you more about the next five years of your costs than the listing ever will.

## There's help, and it's worth asking about

**On the Alabama side,** the Alabama Housing Finance Authority runs down payment programs through participating lenders. The best known is Step Up, which pairs a 30-year fixed first mortgage with a smaller second mortgage that covers part of the down payment. It's built for moderate-income buyers and, despite the reputation, isn't limited to first-timers. There are income limits, credit requirements and a homebuyer education course. The limits change, so get current numbers from AHFA or a participating lender, not a blog post, including this one.

**USDA loans** can work in parts of Baldwin County that most people wouldn't call rural. Eligibility is set by address, so plug the exact address into USDA's eligibility map before you rule it in or out.

**On the Florida side,** the Florida Housing Finance Corporation runs its own first-time buyer programs with their own rules. If you're looking on both sides of the state line, ask your lender about both sets.

**Military buyers** should start with the VA loan conversation. If you're PCSing to NAS Pensacola, I've written separately about the rent-or-buy math on a three-year set of orders.

## Keep cash for after the keys

Down payment and closing costs are the numbers everyone plans for. The one first-time buyers skip is the money you need after closing.

Many coastal policies carry a separate hurricane deductible written as a percentage of the dwelling coverage instead of a flat dollar amount. On a modest house that can still be thousands of dollars out of pocket on the day a storm comes through. Ask your insurance agent to write your deductibles in dollars, then keep that much set aside if you possibly can. Using every dollar you have to close and leaving nothing for the first storm is the version of homeownership that keeps people up at night.

## Where the math tends to work

Being honest about it, a lot of first-time buyers who want to be near the beach end up buying a little inland. Foley, Summerdale, Robertsdale, Loxley and Elberta sit along or near the Highway 59 corridor, close enough that the beach is a weekend habit, with insurance quotes that tend to be gentler than on the island. Brand-new construction in those towns is often the most direct path to a first house with a newer roof, which helps on the insurance side.

That's where the numbers usually point, not a rule. Run them for your situation.

## The short version

1. Get a local pre-approval with a real insurance estimate in it.
2. Price insurance and flood on the exact address before you fall in love.
3. On a condo, confirm the building works for your loan before anything else.
4. Ask about AHFA, USDA and Florida Housing programs before you assume you don't qualify.
5. Keep your hurricane deductible, in dollars, set aside after closing.
