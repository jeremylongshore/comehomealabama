A lot of the new-construction buyers I talk to are planning to skip the home inspection. The reasoning sounds fine. The house is brand-new, the city inspected it, and there's a warranty. What's an inspector going to find?

Usually something. Not because builders here are careless, but because every one of those three reassurances covers less than people assume. Here's what each one actually does.

## What the city sign-off means

The building inspector's job is code compliance. They come out at set stages, check what the code requires at that stage, and sign off. A certificate of occupancy means the house met the minimum standard on the days somebody looked.

It does not mean anyone opened every window, ran every faucet, walked the attic, or checked that the yard drains away from the slab instead of toward it. The inspector isn't working for you. They're working for the code, and the code is a floor.

## What the warranty actually promises

A builder warranty is a document with its own terms, and the terms are the whole story. Many builders split coverage into tiers: a shorter window for workmanship and finishes, a longer one for major systems, the longest for structure. Yours may not look like that. Read the one you're actually getting, before you sign the purchase contract, and look for five things:

- **What's covered and for how long,** item by item.
- **What's excluded.** Settling cracks, grading, landscaping and storm damage are common places to find a carve-out.
- **How you file a claim.** In writing, through a portal, within a set number of days of noticing the problem.
- **Who decides the fix.** Most warranties let the builder choose repair over replacement.
- **Whether it transfers** to the next owner if you sell, and how.

A warranty is a promise to fix things that show up. It isn't a promise that nothing will show up, and it doesn't help with anything you never noticed.

## What the state can and can't do for you

Alabama does regulate home builders. Anyone contracting for residential work over $10,000 needs a license from the Home Builders Licensure Board, and you can check a builder on the Board's Licensee Search or by calling 1-800-304-0853. Do that before you sign anything.

But read the Board's own consumer pages and the limits are plain. The Board can reprimand, fine, suspend or revoke a license. It cannot order a builder to come back and fix your house. It cannot award you money. And it says outright that it cannot discipline a builder for poor workmanship, cosmetic issues or contract disputes.

Two more details matter. Only the original homeowner can file a complaint with the Board, and it has to be within six years of substantial completion or of taking possession. And the Homeowners' Recovery Fund pays at most $30,000 per transaction, only after you've won a court judgment against a licensed builder and couldn't collect.

That's a real safety net, and it's a narrow one. Day to day, your leverage is the warranty document and a clean written record of every problem you reported and when.

## Three inspections, not one

On new construction the inspection isn't a single event. It's timed to the moments when problems are cheapest to fix.

**Pre-drywall, if you're building from the ground up.** Once the walls close, nobody sees the framing, the wiring, the plumbing runs or the window flashing again. Ask before you sign whether the contract allows your own inspector on site at this stage. Some builders permit it with notice. Some don't, and that's worth knowing early.

**Before the final walkthrough.** Book your inspector a few days ahead of closing so their report becomes your punch list. A list a builder agrees to fix before closing is a stronger position than a warranty claim after you've moved in.

**At eleven months.** This is the one people forget. Have the house looked at again shortly before the first-year coverage ends and file everything you find, in writing, while it's still inside that window.

On the coast, make sure the inspector looks hard at the attic and roof attachment, the window and door flashing, and the grading around the slab. If the house is sold as built to a FORTIFIED standard, ask for the certificate itself. It matters for your insurance quote, and a sales sheet is not a certificate.

## Buying a nearly-new resale

A house that's two or three years old looks a lot like new construction, but you're in a different seat. You aren't the original homeowner, so the Board's complaint path isn't yours. Whatever warranty remains is only useful if it transfers and you know exactly how.

So ask the seller for the warranty document, the transfer process, and a list of every claim they filed and how each one was resolved. Then get a full inspection like you would on any resale. Brand-new paint on a three-year-old house tells you nothing about the slab.

## What to actually do

- Look up the builder's license before you sign.
- Get the warranty document itself, not the brochure, and read the exclusions.
- Write your own inspector's access into the contract.
- Inspect before the final walkthrough, and again at eleven months.
- Keep every warranty request in writing, dated.

None of this is a knock on new construction. A lot of buyers come home to the coast in a brand-new house and it's often the right call. It just works better when you treat the warranty as a backstop and the inspection as the plan. Think of the inspector as the one person on the job site who's only working for you.
