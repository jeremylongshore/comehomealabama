People call me about Fairhope after they've already been here once. That's usually how it
goes. They came over for a weekend, walked down Section Street, sat on the bluff at sunset,
and something about it stuck. Then they get home, start looking at houses online, and call
to ask what they're missing.

They're missing two things. Not deal breakers. Just two questions that decide how your
closing goes, and almost nobody asks them until a lender or a title company forces the
issue three weeks in.

## What Fairhope actually is

Start with the geography, because it explains the rest. Fairhope sits on a bluff over the
eastern shore of Mobile Bay. Not the Gulf. The bay. The ground is high, the oaks are old,
the municipal pier runs out into the water at the bottom of the hill, and a few times a
year the bay throws a jubilee and the fish and crabs come to the shallows at dawn.

It's also a genuinely unusual place on paper. Fairhope was founded in 1894 by a group out
of Des Moines led by Ernest B. Gaston, who came down to build a colony around Henry
George's single-tax ideas. That experiment did not fade into a plaque. The Fairhope Single
Tax Corporation still exists as a nonprofit and still holds roughly 4,500 acres in and
around town, per the Encyclopedia of Alabama. It is one of two such colonies left in the
country. The other one is in Arden, Delaware.

Which brings us to the first question.

## One: is the land under that house leased?

If a house sits on colony land, you are not buying the dirt. You buy the improvements,
meaning the house and everything built on the lot, and you take over a renewable 99-year
lease on the land itself. You pay the corporation once a year, and that payment covers the
property taxes on the land plus administration and what they call a demonstration fee,
which funds local projects. That's part of why the town looks the way it does.

This is not a problem. Thousands of people in Fairhope live exactly this way and have for
generations. It is a difference, and differences need to surface early, because two things
change:

1. **Your lender.** A leasehold interest is not the same loan file as a fee simple purchase.
   Some lenders handle it routinely. Some will tell you at day 25 that they don't. Ask
   before you write the offer, not after the inspection.
2. **Your transfer.** The lease has to be assigned through the corporation, and that's a
   step with its own timeline sitting alongside your title work. Their office handles
   transfers directly, so build the call into week one.

The honest version is simple. Ask whether the lot is colony land. If the answer is yes or
"I'm not sure," get it confirmed in writing before you're emotionally committed to a
kitchen.

## Two: is the address actually in the city?

This one catches relocation buyers constantly. A Fairhope mailing address and the Fairhope
city limits are two different maps. The ZIPs, 36532 and 36533, reach well past the
incorporated town, which the city puts at about 14.5 square miles.

The utilities make the same point. Fairhope runs its own electric, gas, water and sewer,
and the city says those departments serve more than 24,000 customers stretching from Bay
Minette down to Fish River. That footprint is far larger than the city itself. So "we're on
Fairhope utilities" does not prove you're in Fairhope, and being outside the line changes
your city taxes, your city services, your voting, and sometimes who shows up when something
breaks.

Ask for the parcel number, then confirm jurisdiction with the city and the Baldwin County
Revenue Commissioner. Ten minutes, and it settles the question permanently.

## The commute, told straight

If somebody in the house works in Mobile, understand the crossing before you sign. I-10
leaves Baldwin County on the Bayway, an eight-mile viaduct over the Blakeley and Tensaw
rivers and the top of the bay, and lands in the George Wallace Tunnel to get downtown. The
tunnel and the Bayway back up routinely, and not only at rush hour.

Plenty of people make that drive every day and think it's a fair trade. The mistake is
pricing your life off a Sunday afternoon test drive. Do it on a Tuesday at 7:30 a.m., in
the rain if you can arrange it, and then decide.

## What Fairhope isn't

It isn't the beach. If your picture of coastal Alabama is white sand and a balcony over the
Gulf, that's a drive from here, and you should look at Gulf Shores or Orange Beach instead
and let me show you what that costs. Fairhope is bay water, a working pier, and a downtown
you can walk. Two different products that happen to share a county.

It also isn't cheap for what you get in square footage. You are paying for the location and
the trees, same as anywhere with a bluff and a hundred-year-old canopy. That's a real
tradeoff, and it's worth saying out loud instead of discovering it at the appraisal.

## Who it fits

Buyers who want walkable over waterfront. Retirees who want the bay, a pier and a bookstore
more than they want a beach chair. Remote workers who need Mobile occasionally instead of
daily. And anyone trading down from a gulf-front condo whose dues and assessments stopped
making sense, because the money often goes further over here and the insurance picture is a
different conversation than a high-rise on the sand.
