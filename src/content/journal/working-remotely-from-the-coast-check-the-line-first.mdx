The call usually goes like this. Someone with a laptop job has found the house. Screened
porch, ten minutes to the beach, the whole picture. They ask about the flood zone, the
roof, the insurance. Good questions. Then, almost as an afterthought: "The internet's
fine out there, right?"

Maybe. That answer depends on the exact address, not the town. For someone whose paycheck
runs through a video call, it's the first question, not the last.

Here's how I'd check it before you fall any further for the porch.

## Check the address, not the zip code

Start with the FCC's National Broadband Map at broadbandmap.fcc.gov. Type in the street
address and it lists the providers that report they can serve that location, along with
the technology and the advertised speeds.

Two cautions. The map reflects what providers report. It isn't a guarantee. And "can
serve" can mean a line has to be run to the house, sometimes at your cost and on their
schedule. If the map is wrong, the FCC lets you file an availability challenge, and the
provider has to respond. That helps the next buyer. It won't help you close on time.

So do two more things. Ask the provider for a serviceability check on the exact address,
in writing. And ask the seller, through your agent, which service they use and what it
actually delivers. A current bill tells you more than an advertisement does.

The picture is shifting in parts of Baldwin County. In October 2024, Point Broadband
bought the residential cable and internet business of Riviera Utilities, based in Foley.
It said it would convert that network to 100 percent fiber and double its footprint in
the area. That's good news in general. It doesn't tell you
what reaches one particular street today. Out on acreage off a county road, your only
options may be fixed wireless or satellite. That can be workable. Just know it before
the offer, not after the movers leave.

## Plan for the storm week

Hurricane season runs through November 30. When the power goes out, your internet goes
with it, however good the line is. The question isn't whether you'll lose power at some
point. It's what your work week looks like when you do.

Find out who supplies electricity at the address. Depending on where you land, that's
Baldwin EMC, Riviera Utilities, a city-run utility, or, across the line in Florida, FPL,
which absorbed Gulf Power at the start of 2022. Different utilities restore power on
different schedules, and neighbors on the same street will tell you more than any
brochure.

Then build a backup. A cellular hotspot on a different network than your home service is
the cheapest one. If you're thinking about a generator, the CDC's rule is plain: run it
outside, at least 20 feet from doors, windows, and vents. Never in the garage, even with
the door up. Put a battery-powered carbon monoxide detector in the house either way. A
permanent standby unit is its own project, with permits through whichever city or county
has jurisdiction, so price it before you count on it.

This isn't meant to scare anybody off. People work from here year-round. The ones who
don't miss a deadline in September planned for the outage in June.

## The airport math is changing this fall

If your job means flying out a few times a quarter, the map just moved. The Mobile Airport
Authority has said commercial service will shift from Mobile Regional, on the far west
side of Mobile, to a new terminal at Mobile International Airport at the Brookley
Aeroplex, with the first flight anticipated by November. Delta, United, and American are
all expected to move over. The terminal opens with five gates.

For anyone living on the Eastern Shore, that puts the Mobile airport on the downtown side
of the bay instead of across town. Pensacola International is the other option, and for
Perdido Key or Foley it may still be the closer one. Confirm opening dates and routes with
the airlines before you book anything. Then drive both airports once in real traffic,
the same way you'd test a commute.

## One small gift

Both sides of the line run on Central time. Alabama and the Florida Panhandle west of the
Apalachicola River share a clock, so a house in Perdido Key and a house in Fairhope won't
change when your 9 a.m. standup starts. Coastal logistics rarely hand you something for
free, so enjoy that one.

## Before you write the offer

1. Run the exact address through the FCC map, and save a screenshot.
2. Get a written serviceability check from at least one wired provider.
3. Ask what the seller actually uses, and what they pay for it.
4. Identify the electric utility, and ask two neighbors how long the last outage lasted.
5. Price your backup, whether that's a hotspot, a portable generator, or a standby unit.

None of this is complicated. It just has to happen in the right order, because a house
that can't hold a video call isn't a home office. It's a very nice place to be
unreachable.
