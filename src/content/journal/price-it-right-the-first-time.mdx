Per realtor.com's county research data through September, 746 listings in Baldwin County
took a price cut last month. Thirty-six raised their price. That's about twenty cuts for
every increase.

I don't share that to scare anybody. I share it because nearly every one of those sellers
started with a number they believed in, and the market corrected them in public. The point
of this post is to help you pick a number the market doesn't have to correct.

## Why the first price matters most

A new listing usually gets its biggest audience in its first couple of weeks. Every buyer
with a saved search in your range gets an alert, every agent with a client looking in your
area takes a look, and the people who've been waiting for the right place see it at once.
That attention doesn't come back at the same strength after a cut.

What a cut gets you instead is a price history. Buyers can see the original list date and
every reduction, and serious buyers check. A house that started at $549,000 and is now
$499,000 reads differently from one that started at $499,000, even though the price today
is the same. One looks like a fair price. The other looks like a seller who might go lower.

Median time on market in Baldwin was 87 days in September. That's already a slow clock.
Price high, and you can add a month or two to it before you're even in the conversation.

## Sold prices are evidence. Asking prices are hope.

The most common mistake I see is pricing off what's for sale. The listing down the street at
$525,000 tells you what that seller hopes. Whether it sells there is an open question, and
with about 17.6 percent of Baldwin listings showing a price cut in September, the answer is
often no.

Here's how I rank comparables, most useful to least:

1. Closed sales of similar homes nearby, typically from the last three to six months. This
   is what buyers actually paid, and it's what an appraiser will lean on.
2. Pending sales. They tell you where the market is right now, though the final price isn't
   public until closing.
3. Active listings. Your competition, not your evidence.
4. Expired and withdrawn listings. Underrated. They show you the price where buyers said no.

And remember the appraisal. If your buyer is financing, the lender's appraiser has to find
closed sales that support the contract price. A buyer who loves the house at an inflated
number still can't close if the appraisal comes in short, and then you're renegotiating with
someone who now knows the number.

## Price for how buyers search

Buyers don't scroll every listing. They set a price ceiling in a search filter, and most set
it on a round number like $400,000, $450,000 or $500,000. A house listed at $505,000 never
shows up for the buyer whose filter stops at $500,000, and that buyer might have paid
$500,000 for it without blinking.

So when your honest number lands just above a round line, take a hard look at listing just
under it. You give up a few thousand dollars on paper and gain every buyer searching the
bracket below. If that draws competing interest, the price can climb back up through offers,
which is a much better problem than a reduction.

## Coastal things that move the number

On this coast, buyers aren't only pricing the house. They're pricing the monthly cost of
owning it, and some of that lives outside the four walls:

- **Insurance.** Roof age, wind mitigation and flood zone all shape the quote a buyer gets.
  Two similar houses on the same street can carry very different premiums, and buyers price
  that difference into their offers.
- **Condo dues and assessments.** If your building has an assessment voted or under
  discussion, the buyer will find it in the documents. Price for it now, or negotiate it
  later from a weaker spot.
- **Flood insurance requirements.** If the lender requires flood coverage, that changes the
  buyer's monthly payment, which changes what they can offer.

A sale with a newer roof or a better flood zone isn't really a comparable to yours. Adjust
for it honestly, or a buyer's agent will do it for you.

## What to do this month

If you're thinking about listing before the snowbirds arrive:

1. Price off closed sales, not listings, and ask your agent for the expired ones too.
2. Check where your number falls against the round-number search brackets.
3. Gather your insurance declarations, roof age and any wind mitigation report so buyers can
   price the monthly cost quickly.
4. If it's a condo, get the budget, reserve study and any assessment in writing before you
   set a price.
5. Decide ahead of time what you'll do if there are no offers in the first three weeks. A
   plan made calmly beats a cut made in frustration.

Nobody lists hoping to show up in next month's count. The surest way to stay out of it is
never needing the cut.
