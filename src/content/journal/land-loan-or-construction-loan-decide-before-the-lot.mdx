A lot of land calls start the same way. Someone found five acres off Highway 59 between Loxley and Robertsdale, or a wooded lot near Elberta, and they want to know what it takes to buy it. My first question is not about the price. It's about the timeline: are you building in the next year, or are you buying the dirt and figuring out the house later?

Those are two different loans, and picking the wrong one can cost you a second closing, a bigger check at the table, or a lot you can't finance the way you planned.

## Path one: buy the land now, build later

If the house is years away, you're shopping for a land loan. Most lenders treat raw land as riskier than a finished house, because there's nothing on it to live in or resell quickly. In practice that usually means a larger down payment, a shorter term, and a higher rate than a regular mortgage. The exact terms vary a lot from lender to lender, which is why local banks and farm credit lenders are often the first calls I suggest. They look at dirt in this county every week.

A few questions to put to any land lender before you write an offer:

1. What down payment do you require on this specific parcel, and does acreage or road frontage change it?
2. Is the rate fixed, and when does the loan balloon or reset?
3. Will you lend on a lot with no utilities at the road yet?
4. If I pay this down and then build, does my equity in the land count toward the construction loan?

That last question matters more than people expect. More on that below.

Paying cash for land is also common here, and it can be a smart move if the house is far off. Just remember that cash in the ground is cash you can't use for clearing, a driveway, a well, or a septic system. Keep enough back to make the lot buildable.

## Path two: buy and build at once

If you're ready to build soon, ask about a construction-to-permanent loan. One loan covers the lot, the construction draws, and the long-term mortgage, with one closing instead of two. That saves you a second round of closing costs and the risk that rates or your own finances change between finishing the house and qualifying for the mortgage.

Fannie Mae's Selling Guide spells out how these single-closing loans work for lenders who sell to it. The construction period can have no single stretch longer than 12 months, and the total can't run past 18 months. When you're buying the lot as part of the deal, the loan-to-value is figured against the lesser of the total purchase price, meaning the lot price plus construction cost, or the "as completed" appraised value of the finished home.

Here's the practical part: the lender is underwriting your builder and your plans too, not just you. Expect them to want a signed contract, plans and specs, a budget, and a builder they're comfortable with. Start that paperwork before you fall for a lot, not after.

## If you already own the land

This is where path one and path two meet, and where owning the lot can work in your favor.

Under the same Fannie Mae guide, if you already hold title to the lot before the first construction draw, the loan is treated as a limited cash-out refinance, and the loan-to-value is figured against the "as completed" appraised value of the lot and the house together. In plain terms, the equity you've built in the land can do a lot of the work a down payment would otherwise do.

Some round numbers, just to show the shape of it. These are an illustration, not a quote. Say you own your lot outright, the house costs $300,000 to build, and the appraiser values the finished lot and home at $400,000. A $300,000 loan against a $400,000 value is 75 percent loan-to-value, with the land standing in for the down payment. Now picture the same buyer still owing $60,000 on a land loan. That payoff rolls into the new loan, and the picture tightens to $360,000 against $400,000, or 90 percent.

Your real numbers depend on the appraisal, the lender, the loan program, and what you still owe. Every lender has its own overlays on top of the guide. But the principle holds: the less you owe on the dirt when you break ground, the easier the building loan gets.

## The questions that come before either loan

Neither loan fixes a lot that can't be built on. Before you pick a lender, get these answered for the actual parcel:

- Who issues the building permit. Unincorporated Baldwin County, a town, or a city can each have a say, so confirm it by parcel number.
- Whether a septic system will work, or whether sewer is available.
- Where power and water actually reach, and what it costs to bring them to the homesite.
- The flood zone, and what that does to your foundation design and insurance.

I covered those in more detail in the land checklist and the utilities post on this journal. A lender will ask most of the same questions, and you'll get a better answer from them if you walk in already knowing.

## The short version

Building within a year? Talk to construction-to-permanent lenders first, and line up your builder before your lot. Building later? Shop land loans carefully, ask how your land equity will count later, and keep cash back for site work. Either way, the loan decision belongs at the front of the process, not after the contract is signed.
