Nobody puts the tag office on the moving checklist. The truck leaves, the boxes get opened, and somewhere in the second or third week somebody notices the out-of-state plate still on the car.

On this coast, that plate has a deadline. Which deadline depends on which side of the state line you closed on, and the two states don't just run different clocks. They charge you in completely different ways.

## When the clock starts

**In Alabama**, the Department of Revenue points to Section 40-12-262 of the Code of Alabama: a personal vehicle with a valid registration from another state has 30 days from the date it enters Alabama to be registered here. Miss it and the state assesses a $15 penalty plus interest. Not ruinous, but it's money for nothing.

Your license runs on a similar clock. Per ALEA, a valid out-of-state license lets you drive for 30 days after you become an Alabama resident. If that license hasn't been expired more than a year, you won't have to take a driving exam to switch.

**In Florida**, the clock is shorter. The state's motor vehicle department (FLHSMV) says new residents have 30 days to get a Florida driver license, but only 10 days to title and register their vehicles, and that 10 days covers boats and mobile homes too.

Florida also spells out what makes you a resident. Taking a job or starting a business here, enrolling a child in public school, registering to vote, filing for a homestead exemption, or living here more than six months in a row. Read that list closely if you're buying on Perdido Key. Filing your homestead, which you'll want to do, starts the 10-day tag clock.

If you're a winter resident keeping your residency up north, these clocks may not be yours yet. Just notice that "more than six months in a row" is on Florida's list, and a long winter can creep up on it.

## The Alabama side: your tag is a tax

In Baldwin County, tags and titles go through the Probate Office's motor vehicle counters in Bay Minette, Fairhope, Foley and Robertsdale. The county recommends booking an appointment, and you can call 251-239-4311 to set one up.

Two things have to be in place first. Your car needs an Alabama liability insurance policy before it can be registered, and if your vehicle requires a title, you apply for an Alabama title as part of the process.

Then comes the part movers don't expect. Alabama charges an annual property tax on your car, collected right at the counter with your registration fees and again at every renewal. A personal vehicle is assessed at 15 percent of its value, which the state calculates, and that assessed value is multiplied by your local millage.

An illustration, not a quote on your car: take a $30,000 vehicle. Fifteen percent is $4,500. Run that through the 33.0 mills Gulf Shores publishes for its property tax and you get about **$148.50** a year. Two cars, call it **$300**, on top of the registration fees themselves. Your district's rate and the state's value for your car set the real number, and it shrinks as the car ages.

## The Florida side: the cost comes up front

Florida does it the other way around. The state constitution says motor vehicles and boats pay a license tax for their operation but "shall not be subject to ad valorem taxes." There's no yearly property tax on your car.

Instead, Florida charges a one-time **$225** fee the first time most cars and light trucks are registered in the state, under Section 320.072 of the Florida Statutes. Two cars coming down from up north means $450 before the regular registration fees. The statute exempts several military situations, including servicemembers reassigned to Florida, so if you're here on PCS orders to NAS Pensacola, bring your orders and ask.

Two more Florida requirements catch people:

- **The VIN has to be physically checked.** A law enforcement officer, a licensed Florida dealer, or a tax collector or state examiner has to see it in person. On Perdido Key, that's the Escambia County Tax Collector's office.
- **Your old insurance won't carry over.** Florida wants a policy from an agent licensed in Florida, with at least $10,000 in personal injury protection and $10,000 in property damage liability.

## The honest comparison

Over a few years, the math evens out more than either side would like you to think. Alabama keeps billing you every year, a little less each time as the car gets older. Florida takes its bigger bite once and then leaves the car alone.

Neither one is a reason to pick a side of the line. Property tax and insurance on the house will dwarf anything you pay on the car. I walked through those in the journal piece on retiring along the state line. But the car costs land in your first month, right when the moving bills are already stacking up, so budget for them.

And if you brought a boat, it has its own registration rules in both states. Ask before the first weekend on the water. The fish will wait. The tag office won't.

## Your first-month list

1. **Call an insurance agent in your new state** the week you close. Both states want local coverage before they'll give you a plate.
2. **Find your title.** If a lender holds it, ask them early how they handle an out-of-state transfer.
3. **Book the appointment.** Baldwin County's Probate Office or, on the Florida side, the Escambia County Tax Collector.
4. **Florida only:** get the VIN verified, and count 10 days from the day you become a resident.
5. **Alabama only:** budget the ad valorem tax on each car.
6. **Switch your license within 30 days** on either side.
7. **File your homestead separately.** In Baldwin County that's before December 31 of the year you bought.

This is a guide to the paperwork, not legal or tax advice, and fees change. Confirm the current numbers with the office before you go.
